How to use Audio Editor

  1. Choose an audio file or a video whose soundtrack you want to edit.
  2. Wait for the browser to decode the file and draw the waveform from its real samples.
  3. Drag the waveform or use the range controls to choose the part you want to keep.
  4. Apply gain, fades, compression, EQ, noise filtering, pitch/speed or cleanup as needed.
  5. Use Play original and Play edited to compare, then Undo or Redo to revisit a setting.
  6. Optionally load background tracks or opt in to the local Whisper model for a transcript.
  7. Download WAV or MP3, or M4A if your browser can encode AAC.

In-depth guide

Edit audio in your browser without an upload

Audio edits are often small: remove a pause at the start, drop the level of a loud intro, fade the ending so it does not cut off abruptly. This editor keeps those jobs in one place and runs them in your browser. Load an audio file or the audio track of a video, watch the waveform draw from decoded samples, then set a trim range and apply gain, fades, compression, EQ, filtering or speed and pitch changes. You can compare the original and processed buffers with A/B playback, undo a pass and export only when it sounds right. Nothing is uploaded to LetitEdit or to a conversion service, so a private recording stays on the device where it was made. The original file is never changed; every edit produces a new download. That means you can try a short fade, compare it with a longer one and keep whichever copy you prefer without damaging the source.

Loading a file and reading the waveform

Choose an audio file such as MP3, WAV or M4A, or select a video file if you want to work with its soundtrack. The browser decodes the file locally and draws a waveform: a picture of signal level over time. Loud passages appear as tall peaks, quiet passages as lower areas, and silence as a flat line. The waveform is your main guide for trimming. A long flat section at the beginning usually means dead air you can remove, while a sharp block of tall peaks may point to a click or an unexpectedly loud word. Zoom and scroll if the tool offers them, because a full recording compressed into a narrow strip hides detail. If decoding fails, the tool reports it rather than showing a fake waveform; that usually means the browser cannot read that particular codec, and converting the source elsewhere is the practical next step.

Trim, gain and fades

Trimming sets the start and end of the piece you keep. Drag the handles on the waveform, then play just the selected region to check that no word is clipped. Gain changes the overall level: raising it makes a quiet recording easier to hear, but pushing too far can introduce distortion, especially on already loud peaks. Lowering gain is safer and often useful when a clip was recorded too hot. Fade in ramps the level from silence at the start, and fade out does the same at the end. They remove the click you get when a waveform begins or ends mid-cycle and make speech sound less abrupt. Use short fades on voice and longer ones on music. Each control can be adjusted independently, so change one at a time and listen again before moving on.

Reverse and playback

Reverse flips the selected audio so it plays backwards. It is mainly a creative effect for short stings and sound design, and it can also make certain speech patterns easier to notice when you are studying them. Because reversing a long track means re-reading the whole buffer, it may take a moment on a large file. Playback is the check that ties everything together. Listen from the beginning of the trimmed region, then jump to the last few seconds to confirm the fade actually reaches silence. Headphones help you hear low-level hiss or a small click that a laptop speaker may hide. If something sounds wrong, reset the last edit rather than stacking more corrections on top: a modest gain change plus a clean trim is usually better than several extreme settings fighting each other.

Export formats and limits

You can download the edited clip as WAV or MP3. WAV is uncompressed and preserves the samples the browser produced, which is useful when you plan to edit the file again or need a clean master. MP3 is much smaller and convenient for sharing, but it is lossy, so quiet detail can soften slightly after saving. M4A is offered only when the browser can encode AAC; if the option is missing, that is a browser limitation, not a broken tool. This editor works on one main clip with up to two locally decoded music layers; it is not a multi-track timeline, and pitch changes are honest resampling rather than voice conversion. Very long recordings use more memory and may be slow to decode and export. Keep the tab active during export, and close or reload the page only after downloading anything you want to keep.

Frequently asked questions

Is my audio uploaded anywhere?

No. Decoding, editing and playback all happen in your browser. The file is not sent to LetitEdit or to an audio conversion service, and closing or reloading the page clears the working copy.

Can I edit the audio from a video file?

Yes. Load a video and the tool extracts its soundtrack locally. Only the audio is edited; this tool does not save a new video file. For picture edits, use the video editor.

Why is M4A sometimes unavailable?

M4A needs an AAC encoder, and not every browser provides one. The option appears only when the browser can encode AAC. WAV and MP3 are the dependable export choices; nothing is wrong with your file if M4A is missing.

What do gain and fade actually change?

Gain raises or lowers the level of the whole selection. Fade in ramps from silence at the start, and fade out ramps down to silence at the end. Fades remove clicks and make edits sound smoother; they do not remove background noise.

Do pitch, speed and voice effects preserve the original voice?

No. Pitch and speed use linear resampling, so duration and tone change together, like a tape-speed control. Telephone, radio, bright and dark options are filter or pitch-style effects, not voice or identity conversion.

How do playback and undo work?

Play original and Play edited use the decoded buffers themselves, not a decorative preview. Each Apply edits operation pushes a real processed buffer onto the undo stack; Undo and Redo restore the earlier state.

Can this remove noise or fix a distorted recording?

It can reduce steady hiss, hum and rumble with high-pass, low-pass and notch filters plus an estimated noise-floor gate. It can also soften peaks with a compressor and soft limiter, but it cannot reconstruct words hidden by noise or recover detail that was never recorded. Compare with Play original before exporting.

Why is a long recording slow to process?

A long file holds a lot of samples, so decoding, drawing the waveform and encoding the export all take more work. Keep the tab active while it runs, close other heavy tabs and try trimming to a shorter region first if the page becomes sluggish.
