    Guide

    What merging audio means

    Merging audio means placing several recordings or songs one after another and writing the result as a single file. Unlike mixing, which usually layers tracks that play at the same time, a merge is mostly about sequence: what comes first, what comes next, and how each boundary sounds. A small overlap or a short silence at a boundary can make the difference between an abrupt cut and a smooth transition.

    This tool keeps every source intact in memory, arranges the tracks in the order you choose, renders the full sequence at the selected sample rate, and then encodes one new file. The original files on your device are never modified. You can change the order, volume, gaps or crossfades and render again without uploading anything.

    How to merge audio files

    1. Add two or more files. Drag them onto the page or choose Browse audio files. MP3, WAV, M4A, AAC, OGG, FLAC and WebM are accepted when this browser can decode them.
    2. Check the order. The track list is the output order. Drag a track by its handle, or use the Move up and Move down buttons. Remove or replace a track without starting over.
    3. Set each boundary. For every adjacent pair, choose a silence gap or a crossfade. A gap can be zero seconds. A crossfade overlaps the end of one track with the start of the next.
    4. Adjust the tracks. Set volume, mute a track, or reset it to 100%. Turn on RMS level matching if the sources were recorded at very different levels.
    5. Preview the complete sequence. The sequence player renders the same mix that the export uses, so gaps, crossfades and volume changes are audible before you download.
    6. Choose the output. Pick WAV or MP3, a sample rate and mono or stereo. Then press Merge and download. The page shows real stages of rendering and encoding, and the download appears only when the file is complete.

    Gaps, crossfades and equal-power fades

    A gap is inserted silence between the end of one track and the start of the next. A gap of zero seconds joins the tracks directly at a sample boundary. A longer gap is useful for spoken introductions, chapter breaks or a deliberate pause between songs.

    A crossfade makes the end of one track overlap the beginning of the next. This tool uses an equal-power curve: the outgoing track follows a cosine fade and the incoming track follows a sine fade. At the midpoint both are at about 70.7% gain, so the perceived energy stays steadier than a simple linear fade, where the middle can sound like a dip. The crossfade duration is capped at half of the shorter track, which prevents a fade from running past the available audio. If a preset is longer than the cap, the page uses the cap and tells you.

    The timeline shows each track as a labelled segment, gaps as pale blocks and crossfades as an overlapping blue region. The full-sequence waveform is drawn from decoded audio peaks and is redrawn from the rendered mix after a preview or export, so it is not a decorative placeholder.

    Track volume, mute and level matching

    Each track has its own volume control from 0% to 150% and a mute button. Volume is applied before the final mix. Muting removes that track's sound but keeps its place in the sequence, so the timing of later tracks does not jump.

    Level matching is optional. When it is on, the tool measures the RMS (root mean square) level of each track, uses the median RMS as the target, and applies a gain of no more than 12 dB in either direction. RMS is a useful, simple measure of average signal energy, but it is not LUFS and this tool does not claim broadcast loudness compliance. After mixing, the tool measures the peak of the finished audio and scales the entire result down only when necessary to prevent clipping. That final safety step is reported in the result details.

    Supported input and output formats

    Input support comes from the browser's own audio decoder. Current Chrome and Edge usually decode MP3, WAV, M4A/AAC, OGG/Opus, FLAC and WebM. Firefox and Safari expose different codec sets, so a file that works in one browser may be rejected in another. AIFF sometimes works. A corrupted file, a DRM-protected file or a codec the browser does not implement is rejected with a message instead of producing a broken result.

    WAV output is uncompressed 16-bit PCM and is always available when the browser can render audio. MP3 output uses a real local LAME encoder and is offered only when the encoder starts and the selected output is mono or stereo at 32 kHz, 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz. When MP3 is not available, the option is disabled and the page explains why; it is never renamed or offered as a format the bytes do not match.

    Sample rate, channels and output size

    Auto mode uses the highest source sample rate among the loaded tracks and outputs mono only when every track is mono; otherwise it outputs stereo. You can also choose 48 kHz, 44.1 kHz, 32 kHz, 22.05 kHz or 16 kHz and force mono or stereo. Changing the output rate does not require decoding the source files again because each source is decoded once at its detected rate and resampled during the offline mix.

    The estimated size is calculated from the chosen format, sample rate, channel count and duration. WAV is predictable but large: 16-bit stereo at 44.1 kHz is about 10.1 MB per minute before the small header. MP3 is much smaller but lossy. The estimate is a guide; the exact byte count is shown after export.

    Privacy, memory and practical limits

    Your audio is decoded, mixed and encoded inside this browser tab. There is no upload, no account, no paid API and no server-side copy. Temporary object URLs are released when you clear the tracks, export again or leave the page. The encoder worker is closed after each export, which releases its buffers.

    The page accepts up to 20 files, 500 MB per file, 1 GB of input in total and 30 minutes of combined decoded audio. The real limit is device memory. A long stereo file at 48 kHz can use hundreds of megabytes when decoded, and rendering another copy needs more. On a phone, prefer shorter tracks and close other memory-heavy tabs. If decoding or rendering fails, the page reports the failure and keeps the tracks you already loaded whenever possible.

    What this tool does not do

    This is a merger, not a full digital audio workstation. It does not record audio, remove noise, change pitch or tempo, split a file into pieces, apply EQ or compression, burn a CD, or edit video. It does not claim LUFS loudness normalization or lossless repair of already lossy sources. Those jobs belong in separate tools. Keeping the scope narrow makes the merge result easier to predict.

    Troubleshooting

    A file will not load. The browser may not support its codec, the file may be corrupted, or it may be too large for the device. Try playing it in the same browser first, then convert it to WAV or MP3 elsewhere if needed.

    The export button is disabled. Add at least two tracks, wait for decoding to finish, or correct a transition that is still being validated. An export already in progress also disables the button.

    MP3 is greyed out. The selected sample rate is not 32, 44.1 or 48 kHz, the output is not mono or stereo, or the local encoder did not start. WAV still works.

    The preview does not start immediately. The first sequence preview has to render the full mix. Longer tracks take longer. Watch the status line; playback starts when rendering finishes.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does the audio merger upload my files?

    No. Every file is decoded, arranged, mixed and encoded inside your browser tab. The audio is not sent to LetitEdit or to a third-party service, and no account is required.

    Which audio formats can I merge?

    MP3, WAV, M4A, AAC, OGG, Opus, FLAC and WebM are accepted when this browser can decode them. AIFF may also work. Support depends on the browser and its codecs, so a format that works in one browser may be rejected in another. A file that cannot be decoded is reported clearly.

    Is the merged file lossless?

    WAV output is uncompressed 16-bit PCM, so no additional lossy encoding is applied. The sources are decoded first, mixed at the chosen sample rate and written as new PCM samples. That is not a bit-for-bit container copy, and a source that was already lossy stays lossy in quality. MP3 output is lossy.

    Can I add a gap or a crossfade between tracks?

    Yes. Each boundary between two adjacent tracks has its own transition. A gap inserts silence from zero seconds upward. A crossfade overlaps the end of one track with the start of the next using equal-power sine and cosine fades. The crossfade is capped at half of the shorter track so it cannot run past the audio.

    What does loudness balancing do?

    When enabled, the tool measures the RMS level of each track, uses the median level as a target and applies a gain of no more than 12 dB up or down. It then measures the peak of the finished mix and scales the whole result down only if needed to prevent clipping. This is RMS matching, not LUFS loudness normalization.

    Can I choose the output sample rate and channels?

    Yes. Auto uses the highest source sample rate and keeps mono only when every track is mono; otherwise it outputs stereo. You can also choose a specific rate and mono or stereo output. MP3 is offered only for mono or stereo at 32 kHz, 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz.

    How many files can I merge at once?

    The page accepts up to 20 files, 500 MB per file, 1 GB of input in total and 30 minutes of combined decoded audio. Device memory is the practical limit, especially on phones, so shorter tracks are more reliable.

    Can I reorder tracks without a mouse?

    Yes. Every track has Move up and Move down buttons that work with a keyboard and a screen reader. A drag handle also supports mouse, pen and touch reordering.

    Does the tool work on phones and tablets?

    Yes. The workspace, track list, controls and timeline are responsive, the controls are touch-sized, and the timeline scrolls horizontally when it is wider than the screen. Long files can still exhaust the memory of a phone.
