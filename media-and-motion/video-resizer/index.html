Guide

What video resizing means

Resizing changes how many pixels represent each frame. A 1920 × 1080 video contains about two million pixels per frame; a 1280 × 720 version contains fewer than one million. The picture is the same scene, but every frame is rebuilt at the new size and encoded again. This is different from changing a player's display size with CSS, which only makes the original file look smaller or larger.

This tool decodes the actual frames, draws them into a new canvas at the chosen output size, and encodes that canvas into a new video file. The downloaded file really is the resolution shown in the result panel. The original file is never modified.

How to resize a video in your browser

  1. Add one video. Drag a file onto the upload area or choose Browse video. MP4, M4V, MOV, WebM and MKV files are accepted when this browser can decode them.
  2. Choose a resolution. Pick 4K, 1440p, 1080p, 720p, 480p, 360p, 240p, Original or Custom. The width and height fields show the exact output size.
  3. Pick the shape. Use Original to keep the source shape, or choose 16:9, 9:16, 1:1, 4:3, 3:2, 2:3, 4:5 or Custom. The preset resolution adapts to the chosen shape instead of stretching the picture.
  4. Decide how it fits. Fit keeps the whole video and pads any leftover area with the background colour. Fill covers the output frame and crops the overflowing edges. Neither mode distorts the image.
  5. Set the background if needed. Black, white and a custom colour are available for Fit. Fill does not use a background because the frame is fully covered.
  6. Choose format and quality. MP4 and WebM are offered only when this browser can encode them at the chosen size. High, Balanced, Smaller file and Custom bitrate control the encoder target.
  7. Resize and download. The page reports real progress, allows a safe cancel, verifies the resulting file and then offers a local download.

Resolution presets and the short edge

The presets are named after the familiar 16:9 sizes: 4K is 3840 × 2160, 1440p is 2560 × 1440, 1080p is 1920 × 1080, 720p is 1280 × 720, 480p is 854 × 480, 360p is 640 × 360 and 240p is 426 × 240. Those exact numbers apply when the output is 16:9.

For other shapes the preset keeps the same short edge and recalculates the other side. A 1080p preset with 9:16 becomes 1080 × 1920, not 1920 × 1080 squeezed into a tall frame. A 1080p preset with 1:1 becomes 1080 × 1080. A 720p preset with 4:3 becomes 960 × 720. This keeps the quality tier consistent while respecting the selected shape.

Original uses the uploaded video's own display size. If you keep the Original aspect ratio, the output is the same size as the source. If you choose another shape while Original is selected, the tool uses the source's short edge as the quality tier and adapts the other side. Custom lets you type both sides directly.

Aspect ratios, Fit and Fill

An aspect ratio is the relationship between width and height. If the output shape matches the source, Fit and Fill produce the same picture. If the shapes differ, the tool has to do something with the extra space or the extra picture:

  • Fit scales the video until the whole frame is visible. The leftover area becomes padding in the chosen background colour. Nothing is cropped.
  • Fill scales the video until it covers the whole output frame. The parts that overflow the frame are cropped. Nothing is stretched.

For example, a 16:9 video placed in a 1:1 frame with Fit becomes a square with black bars above and below. With Fill, the square uses the full height and cuts off the left and right edges. The page states which one will happen before you export, so important content is not removed silently.

The aspect-ratio lock keeps width and height proportional when you edit one of them. Unlock it to enter independent values. Choosing a named ratio locks it again and recalculates the output size.

Common reasons to resize a video

  • Platform limits. Some upload forms accept a maximum width, height or file size, and a smaller export can pass a limit that the original cannot.
  • Social formats. A 9:16 export suits short-form phone video, a 1:1 export suits square posts, and a 16:9 export suits landscape players.
  • Older devices. A 480p or 360p file plays more reliably on slow hardware and uses less storage.
  • Presentations and embeds. Matching a slide, kiosk or website player avoids unnecessary scaling by the player.
  • Consistent project sizes. Several clips can be exported at the same resolution before they are edited together.
  • Smaller archives. A lower resolution usually produces a smaller file, although the encoder settings still matter.

Quality, file size and re-encoding

Resizing always re-encodes. High targets more detail and usually produces a larger file. Balanced is a practical default. Smaller file compresses more aggressively. Custom bitrate lets you set a video bitrate in megabits per second and shows an estimate based on that bitrate and the duration.

The estimate is only shown for Custom bitrate, because the qualitative presets do not promise an exact size. Even with an explicit bitrate, the real file can be smaller or larger: encoders vary their rate, the container adds overhead, and audio has its own bitrate. The actual output size is measured after export and displayed in the result panel.

Upscaling cannot add detail. A 4K export from a 720p source has more pixels but not more real information, and it may look softer while taking longer to encode. Original resolution avoids unnecessary enlargement.

Supported formats and browser limits

MP4, M4V, MOV, WebM and MKV inputs are accepted when the browser can decode their video and audio codecs. Current Chrome and Edge usually handle H.264/AAC MP4, VP8/VP9/Opus WebM and many MOV, M4V and MKV files. Firefox and Safari expose different codec sets, so the same file may work in one browser and fail in another.

Output is limited to MP4 and WebM. MP4 uses H.264 video and AAC audio; WebM uses VP9 video and Opus audio. The format selector is checked against the browser's WebCodecs encoders for the exact output size. A format that cannot be encoded at that size is disabled with an explanation instead of producing a broken download.

Encoders generally require even width and height. When a preset, ratio or custom size produces an odd number, the tool rounds that dimension to the nearest even pixel and says so. The output is capped at 4096 pixels on either edge.

Audio, rotation and frame timing

Audio is decoded separately, normalized to 48 kHz stereo, and written alongside the resized video with its original timing. A video without an audio track exports without one. If a file has audio that this browser cannot decode, the file is rejected with a clear message; the tool does not silently drop the sound. If the browser cannot encode the selected audio codec, that output format is disabled.

Rotation metadata is applied while the frames are decoded, so the output shows the same orientation you see in the preview and does not carry a misleading rotation flag. Decoded frame timestamps are kept, so variable-frame-rate sources are not flattened into a fixed rate and the duration stays aligned with the source.

Privacy and performance

The video is decoded, resized, encoded and muxed inside a Web Worker in this tab using Mediabunny and the browser's WebCodecs implementation. The worker is loaded only after a video is added. It is terminated when you remove the video or leave the page, which releases decoder and encoder resources.

Files can be up to 2 GB, but that is a safety limit, not a promise that every device can process a file that size. Long or high-resolution videos need more memory. The page warns when a file is large or the output is very large. If an export fails, try a shorter video, a smaller output size, or close other memory-heavy tabs.

There is no upload, account, paid API or server-side copy. Temporary object URLs for the source and the result are released when you replace the video, start another resize or leave the page.

Troubleshooting

  • The file is rejected. The browser may not decode its codecs, the file may be corrupted, or it may be audio-only. Try an MP4 or WebM that plays in the same browser.
  • A format is disabled. The browser cannot encode that codec at the current output size. Choose the other format or a smaller resolution.
  • The export is slow. 4K and long videos take longer. Keep the tab open, close other memory-heavy tabs and try 1080p or 720p first.
  • The export runs out of memory. Use a shorter clip or a smaller output, especially on phones.
  • The preview will not play. The browser may lack the playback codec even when the exporter can decode it. The result panel will still let you download the file.
  • The output is larger than expected. Re-encoding can increase size, especially when the source was already heavily compressed. Try Smaller file or a lower custom bitrate.

Frequently asked questions

Does the video resizer upload my video?

No. The video is decoded, resized, re-encoded and muxed inside your browser tab. It is not sent to LetitEdit or to a third-party service, and no account is required.

Which video formats can I resize?

MP4, M4V, MOV, WebM and MKV are accepted when this browser can decode their video and audio codecs. Unsupported, corrupted, oversized or audio-only files are rejected with a clear message.

Does resizing change the video's aspect ratio?

Only when you choose a different aspect ratio. Fit keeps the whole picture and adds padding; Fill covers the frame and crops the edges. The tool never stretches the picture to force it into a new shape.

What is the difference between Fit and Fill?

Fit scales the video so the entire frame is visible. If the output shape is different, the leftover area is filled with the chosen background colour. Fill scales the video so it covers the whole output frame and crops the parts that overflow. Fill removes some of the picture.

Is the resized video lossless?

No. Resizing changes the pixels in every frame, so the video is decoded and re-encoded. The High, Balanced and Smaller file options change the encoder quality target. The tool does not claim a lossless stream copy.

Why are the output dimensions rounded to even numbers?

Common video encoders require even width and height values. When a preset, aspect ratio or custom size produces an odd value, the tool rounds it to the nearest even pixel and explains the change. The picture is not stretched.

Will resizing to 4K improve the quality?

No. Upscaling adds pixels but cannot recover detail that the original recording did not contain. A 4K output from a smaller source may look softer and will usually be larger. Choose the original resolution or a smaller size when you want to avoid upscaling.

Is the audio preserved?

Yes, when the browser can decode and encode the audio. Audio is normalized to 48 kHz stereo and keeps its original timing. If the audio cannot be decoded, the file is rejected instead of silently losing its sound.

How do I keep the whole video without black bars?

Use the Original aspect ratio and Fit. The output then has the same shape as the source, so no padding or cropping is needed. A different output shape either needs padding (Fit) or removes part of the picture (Fill).

Can I set a custom bitrate?

Yes. Choose Custom bitrate and enter a value in megabits per second. The page shows an estimate based on that bitrate and the video duration. The real file size can differ because encoders vary their rate, so the actual size is reported after export.

Does the resizer work on phones?

The workspace becomes a single column on small screens, with touch-friendly controls and a responsive preview. Export speed depends on the phone's decoder and encoder support and on available memory.
